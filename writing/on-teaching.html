On Teaching Now
ROUGH DRAFT

I've lately been inclined to write on what it'll mean to teach at a university in the back half of the 2020s. It's been just three years since I started teaching full time, and as of summer 2026 I feel I have no idea what I'm doing or (perhaps more importantly) why I'm doing it.

It's hard to call it an elephant in the room──the idiom presumes the inhabitants of said room are ignoring said elephant. Truth is we can't stop talking about the elephant. Every conversation now takes the form: "I liked that restaurant you recommended, but the space was a bit claustrophobic. Kind of like how this elephant is making it hard to move around in this room." or "Been reading Kafka, it's fascinating. Can you imagine the elephant in this room writing stories like that? Oh, it already did? Wow, I thought I would've heard about it given the elephant is in the room."

The other truth is: my opinion doesn't matter. AI is changing the way that people learn, and──to my mostly quiet frustration──this behooves me to re-evaluate the way I teach.

This essay serves three purposes:

  1. I wanted to organize my thoughts about teaching in 2026, as it relates to AI and the university as an institution.
  2. I wanted to provide an open letter of sorts to students and to the community writ large with the purpose of justifying some of my course-organizational decisions. I hate doing some of the things I do, and I wanted to make a case for why I do them.
  3. I wanted to have some fun writing and ruminating. It's been a while since I've written an essay proper.

What does it mean to learn with AI? I've been considering this the way one considers trying out rock climbing.1 The appeal is clear (to the right kind of person) but it's also kinda questionable right? There's a lot of "stuff" involved, there's like, shoes and chalk and ropes? And you have to pay exorbitantly for a membership out here in Boston…

The other thing (and it pains me to say write this): a lot of people climb. It's become a crowded space. And climbers stereotypically push climbing on their friends. It's the most fun way to get a good workout. Sure, you can get injured but there's a "right" way to do it.

And it isn't all that important to the analogy, but climbing also has levels and subcultures the way that AI use does currently. There are the 5-days-a-week-ers, the once-a-blue-moon-ers, the free-day-pass-ers, the weekend warriors, the board bros, the trad dads, the surprisingly strong STEM kids…2

Where the analogy breaks down in general is where it remains true (for now) in my particular case: no one's telling you that you'll lose your job if you don't climb. The fear of "falling behind" looms large──especially in mathematics, the area with which I'm most familiar──and may be the real elephant in the room.3

If you've lost the track of the point, fair, I think I did too. Let me try get at it again, with concision this time: I don't know what it means to learn with AI and I have no strong desire to know.4 This feels like an admission more than anything else. And it feels like a bad take. I should want to know how a large (perhaps the largest) contingent of students learn. And I should be taking advantage of this new way of learning to expand the scope of my courses. I should be preparing students for the AI-laden "real" world. And I'm being told all this by senior members of my community.

What is my job? The question, as I understand it,5 has two parts. First, what is my objective? Then, how do I accomplish my objective?

Things seem pretty clear to me objective-wise: when <student> finishes my course, <student> knows how to do <topic>. The ambiguity of the "do" is where the discussion begins. Is doing <topic> the same as doing <topic> with the help of an outside resource (e.g., textbook, friend, notes, AI)? Is doing <topic> the same as doing <topic> in the "real" world? What if you'll never "realistically" do <topic>?

This part of the question is ultimately about evaluation. And I'm generally of the opinion that evaluation is an important feature of the university as an institution. This is why I've never been able to get entirely behind ungrading.6 No matter how inclusive the pedagogy, no matter how oppressive the education system, there is in my opinion immense value in receiving feedback from an expert, even if what you choose to do with it is exasperatedly throw up your hands and shout: "The experts have fallen, it shows in their inane, quibbling evaluation. But no matter, the new generation will soon rise!"7

I'd like to divorce the opinion of the previous paragraph from the application of evaluation as a certificate. This is an ugly emergent property of the university as a business. And though I don't believe becoming a business is inherent to the nature of the university──even in a capitalist hellscape──it's not hard to connect the dots; money motivates.

This brings me to one sub-point: I need to evaluate <student> on their ability to do <topic>. And I believe this brings value to <student>, both in time and after the fact. That my evaluation carries weight for future employers of <student> is the moralistic cross I bare as a result not of evaluation-as-such but of the above evaluation-as-certificate point. Every semester I bare this cross with admittedly near-absolute emotional despair as I try to make my evaluation as fair and reasonable as possible, dealing with broken systems, actors of varying degrees of nefarious, and unspoken but incredibly visible power dynamics; it's a veritable mess, and I end every semester in something of a stupor. Perhaps it could be otherwise, but I consider this the sacrifice I make to shield students (whether they want it or not) from the university-as-business, in the hope of imbuing the evaluation we give each year with an iota of academic and intellectual purity.

There's a whole lot more I could say write, but as a meta-point, it seems the contemporary essay often feels the need to say write everything, rather than leave some for a later conversation,8 hence the number of mega-opinion pieces I've recently been sent.9

An aside: above I've made the classic error of conflating feedback and evaluation, where "evaluation" may be read somewhat euphemistically here as "grade". This is partially on purpose; I believe a grade should ideally be treated as a low-fidelity, summarizing form of feedback.10 But I do want to make clear that, no matter the ultimate point I make here, I still consider providing productive feedback high pedagogical priority.

Back to the second part: how do I accomplish said objective? (And what does this have to do with "learning with AI"?) This is invariably the "fun" part, where actual pedagogy meets the metaphorical pavement. I think it best to broad-stroke this one: it's the job of the teacher it to create an environment in which <student> can become competent in <topic>. I like this framing because it says virtually nothing (though not entirely nothing). To loosely quote my advisor/first teaching mentor:

Every pedagogical method works, because in attempting to implement the method, you've thought carefully about the learning environment, and thinking carefully about the learning environment leads to better learning outcomes.

Teaching is a relentlessly personal endeavor. I love discussing pedagogy with fellow teachers, but I think we're sometimes under-conscious of the fact that our conversations always go the same way: "I did <pedagogical method>…oh cool, I tried <other pedagogical method>…interesting, I did something similar but more like <different pedagogical method>…I like that, that's like this thing I did where…" The environmental view of teaching elicits just enough "aura" in the endeavor to justify how self-centered we are; we share in an objective (to the degree that we agree on the objective) but there's an aesthetic we're after in our classrooms, the unnameable thing that sets up the synapses for further growth and stimulation, even and especially post-"business hours".

And like anything with an aesthetic (almost mystic) bent, different folks have different strengths. Course organization is one of my strengths, but I'm pretty weak when it comes to being open to approaches to learning outside of my own.

Okay, let's round that corner at third. AI is changing the way that people learn. I don't care to know how (at the moment) because, frankly, I don't want to (at the moment). But I'd like to be open to the use of AI for learning.11 It's here to stay and (feel free to fight me on this one the most) the evil fog in which the discourse places it will eventually dissipate, not necessarily for it being less evil, but for it being normalized.

Another aside: I've had quite a few conversations with teachers about the most obvious follow-up question: How do students actually use tools like ChatGPT and Claude? This is particularly pertinent as individual teachers and whole institutions are trying to draw circles around "reasonable"/"responsible" AI use. It's difficult to gauge because responding honestly to this question might mean admitting (accidentally or otherwise) to unreasonable/irresponsible use. There's a sense among pedagogues that students are one-shot-ing problem sets, essays, and projects, then f***ing off and snickering on their way out the metaphorical door. Or rather, there's a sense among pedagogues that other pedagogues feel this way. It's implicit in the way we talk about (and, yes, I'm am also guilty of this) "trusting" our students. But, barring the most jaded among us, the word "trust" is mostly a placeholder, we lack a better term. Part of my aim is to make my argument without appealing to trust.

I think it's time for a pseudo-syllogism.

  • People are learning with AI.
  • I don't know what it means to learn with AI, and I don't care to know at the moment. In particular, I'm in no position to judge "fair" or "reasonable" use of AI.
  • Like me, some people have no interest in learning with AI.
  • For a graded form of evaluation to be fair and reasonable, the difference in distribution for AI-users vs. AI-non-users should be statistically insignificant.
  • For AI-users, the solutions to every at-home assignment or project I've written and will ever write in any introductory math or computer science course are available, in perpetuity.
  • Therefore, at-home assignments and projects are no longer a fair and reasonable form of evaluation.

This potential paradigm shift is eviscerating. I love writing problems sets. I love challenging problems, the kinds of problems that feel like puzzles, that you have to put down and come back to. This is almost certainly because working on problems sets was my favorite part of being a student. I loved letting a problem steep; getting stuck, thinking about it on my walk to class, getting angry about it, talking with friends about it.12

That, and also, test anxiety is currently at a high point. It's difficult for me to justify a teaching plan with more quizzes and exams; hence the essay.

I still wholly believe in the at-home assignment as a form of feedback, both in the self-determination of how well you understand something by doing it, and in the targeted feedback we provide; a complete loss of the form of thinking exercised by at-home assignments would sound the final note of my career as a teacher. But I can no longer in good conscience treat the associated grade as meaningful.13

All of the above is predicated on fact that I have near sole determination of my courses' objectives. And unlike some of my peers, I don't believe my job is to preparing <student> for the job market, at least not directly. Nor do I believe that learning with AI helps in this respect.14 In the future, my expertise as a pedagogue may be trumped by the powers that be, who view AI as a necessary part of human education. This doesn't seem so unlikely, with token-surveillance becoming standard practice at high-profile software companies. Until then (at which point, sayonara) I consider competency in <topic> my soul objective.15

But, as I hope I've preempted sufficiently well, my real job begins in deciding how to achieve this. And in the presence of learning with AI, this begs the obvious question: If everything I teach can be presented in uniform way by AI, if AI can write you a custom textbook, a custom syllabus, and answer your every question as you learn, providing you with practice problems and immediate feedback, what do I contribute? This is the "why" of it all. And I don't have a compelling answer; for some folks my AI counterpart is "good enough".

I hesitate to make claims about what AI can't do; it's a game I'm destined to lose, at least eventually. But if pressed: "What do you bring that AI does not?" I'd likely point to two things: story-telling and contrarianism.

As you can tell, I'm not an amazing writer. I'm a wannabe mash-up of Salinger and Wallace, with traces of Russell's strict grammarian. But I've taken a less-traveled intellectual path, one that's shaped me into a "peculiar" writer, presenter, and teacher (and that allows for that obnoxious combination of name-drops). To me this is an integral aspect of story-telling: your life's trajectory is everything. And I'm well aware my brand of intellectual soup isn't for everyone, but I'm also aware I can't be different. This is yet another AI tradeoff: when every detail of everything unfurls completely before you, nothing seems more important than another thing.

Contrarianism follows a similar vein. The first thing I do when I try to learn something is disbelieve it. The second thing I do (after I believe it) is find a better way to present it. Because there's always a better way of presenting it.

Can AI be contrarian? This isn't a dig at the sycophantic tendencies of current out-of-the-box models. AI can disagree, and it can do so aggressively, but is this to be contrarian? There's a philosopher out there who can put me in my place, but in lieu of this schooling, I'd argue that being contrarian depends as much on context as story-telling; it's difficult to interpret someone's acting like an ass without also knowing and understanding them, at least a little.

This is all to say (in a roundabout way): I'm going to bring up infinite dimension spheres and Flatland in my introductory linear algebra course. I'm going to s*** talk Python all the way through my programming languages course, and tell you about how Robin Popplestone drowned his boat when we cover higher-order programming. I'm going to make you learn about tensors in my PL course because I wanted to learn about tensors, and I'm going to make dumb mistakes my students are going to call me out on.

AI is going to tell you that finding singular values is like "treasure hunting" and use the "tip of the iceberg" metaphor every f***ing time it presents a topic that's a little more complicated than it looks at first glance.16

It's about time to dismount my soapbox atop this high horse. But first I feel I should say write a couple lines about why I choose to learn without AI.

AI is often compared in the arena of learning to calculators and Google. Both of these tools changed the way we learn and think, but AI encroaches on the domain of learning in a way that neither of these did.

Calculators changed the rate at which we calculate, but they didn't drastically change what we calculate. Google changed the rate at which we could attain facts about the world, but it didn't drastically change what facts can be known. Both technologies are accompanied by atrophy, but in skills that we could in principle do; with some practice, I can read off a multiplications table, or look up a topic in an encyclopedia.

AI asks us to apply it to tasks for which the "in principle" isn't important; this is kind of it's whole modus operandi. Make a webapp in minutes, you don't even need to know how it works to code. And there are certainly applications of this, e.g., being a better manager/capitalist. But if calculators are the carbon fiber plates of the running world, then LLMs are inline skates. We get to point B more efficiently, but we're not running anymore; we've given up the sport, and traded it for something potentially rewarding, but ultimately less adaptable.

But AI is a tool, you can learn "the old-fashioned way" and still use it as a tool. Maybe. My whole point to some degree is I'm open to this prospect. But learning is an act of reflection, and teaching is an act of sharing. Using AI is an act of taking. You can take answers (this is the definition of cheating) but you can't take comprehension. What if I have a question and no one is around to answer it? Then ask AI. But also consider: confusion is more aid than barrier to learning (also, don't confuse confusion with ignorance).
